A stock's 200-week moving average — roughly its price averaged over the last three and a half years — is a slow-moving line long-term holders watch as a support level. This page tracks, market-wide, how many stocks are trading close enough to their own 200-week line to call it a test, and how many have broken clean below it. It is a support-testing analogue of the breadth line on the front page: not which way the market is going, but how much of it is sitting right at a level that has mattered before.

Fig. I

Share of the Market Near Its Line

% of stocks within ±5% of their own 200-week average · one point per refresh

Each point is one day's reading across the whole usable universe. A stock needs about 3.8 years of trading history to have a 200-week average at all, so recently listed names are excluded rather than estimated — the same honest omission the Valuation page makes for companies its models can't value. There is no backfilled history before this page's first deploy: the line lengthens by one point every time the daily breadth job runs.

Note

Terms of the Reading

One line per stock · same weekly convention as the Stochastic and Shinohara panels

The line. A simple average of the last 200 weekly closes, on the dividend/split-adjusted price so an ex-dividend day never reads as a fake drop toward the line. Weeks are bucketed the same way as the front page's weekly panels — a week's close is whatever the latest available price is, including a week still in progress, not only a week that has fully finished.

Near. Within 5% either side of the line, in either direction. Close from above and close from below both count — the reading is about proximity to the level, not which side a stock sits on.

Not a signal. Sitting near a 200-week average has no fixed meaning by itself — it can be a level that holds, or one that gets cut straight through. This page counts proximity; it does not predict which way it breaks.
